Off-the-shelf software makes the most sense when your process looks like everyone else's: accounting, payroll, invoicing, email, basic financial control. Someone has already solved the problem, many companies use the product, and the vendor handles updates. Paying for a subscription is usually cheaper and faster than building from scratch.

Custom software makes sense when the process is what sets your company apart, when the tools on the market force your team to work around them with spreadsheets and rework, or when you need to bring together steps that today live in separate tools. In those cases, the development investment pays off through efficiency and control over the operation.

In practice, many companies don't have to pick one side. A common path is to keep off-the-shelf software where it works well and build only what is missing: an integration, a module, a dashboard. The decision comes down to three main factors: cost over time, process fit and how dependent you become on a vendor.

## What each option is

**Off-the-shelf software**, also called packaged software or SaaS (software as a service), is a product built to serve many companies at once. You buy a license or subscription, configure it and start using it. The vendor decides how the product evolves.

**Custom software** is built for one specific company's process. Rules, screens and reports reflect the way that operation actually works. The company decides what goes in, what stays out and in what order. Depending on the contract, it also owns the source code.

## When off-the-shelf is the best choice

- The process is standard across the market and gives you no competitive edge, such as accounting, payroll or e-signatures.
- There is a product that covers most of your needs without heavy customization.
- You need to be up and running in days or a few weeks.
- There are legal requirements that change often, such as tax rules, and the vendor takes care of the updates.
- The number of users is small and the subscription fits the budget for years to come.

## When custom software is worth it

- The process is specific to your business, and existing tools force the team to work in a worse way.
- There are lots of spreadsheets, parallel controls and retyping between tools.
- The operation depends on rules that market products don't cover, such as your own pricing calculations, specific approval flows or integration with equipment.
- The software is part of the product you sell to your customers.
- License costs grow with the number of users to the point of exceeding the cost of running your own system.

## Cost over time

The fair comparison is not "monthly subscription versus project quote." It is the total cost over several years, counting everything each path requires.

### With off-the-shelf software

- License or subscription, usually charged per user, per module or by volume.
- Implementation, configuration and training.
- Price increases and plan changes set by the vendor.
- Indirect costs: time spent working around limitations and keeping parallel spreadsheets.
- Integrations with other systems, which are often charged separately or require development.

### With custom software

- Initial development, which concentrates the investment at the start.
- Hosting and infrastructure.
- Corrective and evolutionary maintenance: bug fixes, security updates, rule changes and new features.
- Your own team's time to define requirements and review deliveries.

The cost of custom software doesn't end at launch. All software needs maintenance: libraries get security patches, browsers change, business rules evolve. A budget that ignores this is incomplete.

On the other hand, the cost of off-the-shelf software tends to grow with the company, because more users and more modules mean a bigger subscription. With few users and a standard process, off-the-shelf usually wins. With many users or a very specific process, the math can flip.

## Process fit

Fit is how well the software matches the way the company works. No off-the-shelf product fits perfectly. The question is whether the gap is small and acceptable or whether it creates an ongoing cost.

Sometimes adapting the process to the software is a good idea. Mature products carry practices tested by many companies, and implementation can be a chance to get your house in order. In other cases, the process is exactly what makes the company sell more or deliver better. Changing it to fit the tool means giving up that advantage.

A practical way to assess fit: list the ten most frequent tasks in your operation and ask to see each one done in the candidate software during a demo. If several require extra steps, exports or controls outside the system, the fit is poor.

## Vendor dependency

Every path creates some dependency. What changes is on whom, and how much control you keep.

With off-the-shelf software, you depend on the vendor for pricing, roadmap (the plan for new features), availability and access to your data. Before signing, ask:

- Can you export all your data in an open format, such as a CSV file or through an API?
- What happens to your data if the contract ends?
- Is there an API to integrate with other systems?

With custom software, you depend on the team that builds and maintains it. To reduce that dependency:

- Make sure the contract gives you ownership of the source code and access to the repository.
- Require basic documentation of the architecture, deployment and business rules.
- Prefer mainstream technologies with plenty of available developers over obscure solutions.
- Keep hosting and domain accounts in the company's name.

## The hybrid path: off-the-shelf software with custom modules

For many companies, the most balanced answer is to combine both. The ERP (enterprise resource planning system), the finance tool or the e-commerce platform stay as market products. Custom development covers the parts the product doesn't handle well:

- Integrations that eliminate retyping between systems. See how [system integration with APIs](/en/blog/system-integration-with-apis/) works.
- Automation of repetitive tasks, such as generating documents, sending notifications or consolidating reports. The guide on [how to automate business processes](/en/blog/how-to-automate-business-processes/) shows where to start.
- Portals for customers or partners that pull data from the main system.
- Dashboards that combine metrics from several sources.

This model lowers the initial investment, takes advantage of what the market has already solved and focuses development where it makes a difference. The condition is that the off-the-shelf software offers a reliable way to integrate, ideally through an API.

> Before choosing between off-the-shelf and custom, ask: which part of the process really needs to be different?

## Questions to help you decide

1. Does the process this software will support set your company apart, or is it the same as anyone else's?
2. Is there a product that covers most of your needs without parallel spreadsheets?
3. How many people will use the system today and in a few years, and how does the subscription grow with them?
4. Does the off-the-shelf product let you export your data and integrate with other systems?
5. Do you have budget for the initial investment and for ongoing maintenance of your own system?
6. Who inside the company will define requirements and review deliveries?

If the answers point toward custom software, one way to reduce risk is to start with an [MVP](/en/blog/mvp-how-to-turn-a-software-idea-into-a-product/), a minimal version with the essential features, and evolve from real usage. A [well-written project brief](/en/blog/how-to-write-a-software-project-brief/) helps you get proposals you can actually compare.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is custom software always more expensive?

In upfront investment, usually yes. Over the years, it depends. With few users and a standard process, off-the-shelf software tends to cost less.

With many users, expensive licenses or a lot of rework caused by poor fit, your own system can end up cheaper.

### Can I start with off-the-shelf software and switch later?

Yes, and it's a common path. To make the switch easier, choose a product from the start that lets you export all of your data.

### Who owns the code of custom software?

It depends on the contract. Ideally, the company paying for the project owns the source code and has access to the repository, so it isn't locked into a single vendor. This should be written into the proposal before the project starts.

### What about personal data protection?

Either way, the company remains responsible for the personal data it handles. With off-the-shelf software, check where the data is stored and what the contract says about security. With custom software, data protection has to be planned into the project. In Brazil this is governed by the LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados), the country's data protection law, similar in spirit to Europe's GDPR; the article on [LGPD for websites and software](/en/blog/lgpd-brazil-data-protection-for-websites-and-software/) covers it in depth. Elsewhere, check the rules that apply in your country.
